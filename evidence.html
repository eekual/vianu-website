Evidence
& Sources

Every performance claim on the VIANUcycle site is referenced here with its primary source. Where the science is nuanced, mixed, or limited, we say so. We believe transparency about what the research does — and doesn't — prove is more useful than a clean narrative that oversimplifies it.

Editorial note on the evidence The research on cycling insoles is genuine but not uniform. Some claims are backed by peer-reviewed, cycling-specific RCTs. Others draw on biomechanical first principles or industry sources. The "up to 10% efficiency gain" figure cited on the main site originates from coaching literature citing a Journal of Sports Sciences study — the actual peer-reviewed data shows more modest but still meaningful improvements (2.9–6.8% in specific metrics). We surface that nuance here so you can judge for yourself.
Claim 01

Power & Efficiency Gains

“Proper foot support can increase pedaling efficiency by up to 10%” — VIANUcycle hero stat
Supported in direction, contested in magnitude

The 10% figure is widely cited in cycling coaching literature (Crank Cycle Coaching, G8 Performance) and attributed to the Journal of Sports Sciences. The peer-reviewed studies paint a more nuanced picture: real power gains are documented but typically in the 2–7% range depending on the metric and rider profile. The biomechanical mechanism — that arch collapse during the pedal stroke dissipates energy that would otherwise reach the pedal — is well-supported across multiple independent studies. A 2010 study showed peak power increases of 2.9% and mean power increases of 3.8% with custom insoles. A 2014 PMC study recorded a 6.8% reduction in biceps femoris muscle activation time, indicating improved neuromuscular efficiency. For riders with significant foot pronation or arch collapse, gains toward the upper end of the range are plausible. For biomechanically neutral riders, gains will be smaller. We stand by the claim as directionally accurate; the “up to” qualifier is important.

Cycling Performance Enhancement and Injury Prevention Using an Arch Support Insole with Forefoot Wedge
Peer-Reviewed
Yang et al. (2014) — PMC / NCBI — PMC4101557
Arch support insoles reduced biceps femoris muscle activation time by 6.8% (p=0.005), decreased peak knee sagittal plane moment, and increased cycling efficiency. The study concluded that proper cycling orthotics with arch support and forefoot wedge reduce overuse musculoskeletal injury risk.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101557
The Effect of Foot Orthoses and In-Shoe Wedges During Cycling: A Systematic Review
Systematic Review
Bousie et al. (2013) — PMC / NCBI — PMC4064526
Reviewed multiple studies on foot orthoses in cycling. Orthoses and wedges are believed to be especially beneficial in preventing overuse injuries of the knee and improving power output in cyclists. Notes that improving lower limb alignment seeks a more linear cycling motion — reducing energy lost to lateral forces.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4064526
Custom Cycling Insoles — Power Increase: Peak Power +2.9%, Mean Power +3.8%
Industry Reference
Cited by Momentum SIC (2010 source) — momentumsic.com/custom-cycling-insoles
Riders felt more support in their foot arch and were able to exert greater peak forces. A 2010 study cited in this review suggested cyclists enjoyed an increase in Peak Power of 2.9% and Mean Power of 3.8% with custom insoles. A 2004 study showed foot orthoses prevented arch collapse, resulting in increased pedal efficiency and better knee control.
momentumsic.com/custom-cycling-insoles
Orthotic Bicycle Shoe Insoles Show No Effects on Leg Muscle Activation or Performance in Recreational Cyclists
Peer-Reviewed (Contradicting)
Signofile et al. (2017) — Footwear Science, Vol 9 No 3, pp.139–147
Nine recreational cyclists tested four arch heights. No significant differences were found in muscle activity, pedaling mechanics, or physiological work. Important caveat: small sample, recreational cyclists only — the biomechanical case for insoles is strongest in riders with measurable arch collapse or foot asymmetry.
tandfonline.com — Footwear Science 2017
Claim 02

Knee Injury Prevention

“Corrects tibial rotation and reduces frontal-plane knee drift” — Benefits section, Road & Track
Well-supported by peer-reviewed literature

This is among the strongest claims in the body of cycling orthotic research. Excessive knee varus/valgus forces and axial tibial rotation are widely accepted as primary causes of knee pain in cyclists. Multiple independent studies show that custom orthotics and wedges demonstrably alter frontal-plane knee kinematics. A 2013 study (Yang) demonstrated that foot orthotics optimise foot positioning in the frontal plane during pedaling by improving lower limb alignment — reducing both extreme knee-varus and knee-valgus. The knee receives up to half the rider's body weight with each stroke, repeated up to 5,700 times per hour.

Intervention at the Foot–Shoe–Pedal Interface in Competitive Cyclists
PMC Open Access
O'Neil et al. (2016) — PMC / NCBI — PMC4970853
Excessive, or sub-optimal non-driving moments — knee varus/valgus forces, axial tibial internal or external rotation — are largely considered responsible for knee pain in cyclists. Multi-planar kinematics can be altered using orthotics and wedges to correct aberrant knee motion in cycling.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4970853
Potential Factors Associated With Knee Pain in Cyclists: A Systematic Review
Systematic Review
Dettori & Norvell review (cited) — PMC5973630
Patellofemoral inflammation in cyclists is linked to saddle position, cleat adjustment, and foot mechanics. Use of orthotics is among the preventive strategies recommended by the majority of authors in the literature, alongside saddle adjustment and gear ratio reduction.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5973630
The Efficacy of Arch Support Sports Insoles in Increasing Cycling Performance and Injury Prevention
Peer-Reviewed
Yang (2013) — ResearchGate / Semantic Scholar
Foot orthotics optimise positioning of the foot in the frontal plane during the pedaling movement by improving lower limb alignment. Better alignment reduces the risks of both extreme knee-varus and knee-valgus, thereby preventing injuries while increasing performance.
researchgate.net — Yang 2013
Claim 03

Hot Spots & Plantar Pressure

“Metatarsal pressure distributed evenly — eliminating burning and numbness” — Benefits section, Road & Track
Directly supported by peer-reviewed evidence

Hot spots and numbness in the forefoot are among the most common complaints of cyclists, caused by concentrated plantar pressure on the metatarsal heads during the power phase of the pedal stroke. The research here is particularly strong — insole material and contour demonstrably alters plantar pressure distribution. A 2023 PMC crossover study found that hard insoles with metatarsal head reinforcement significantly decreased maximum plantar pressure in all test areas compared to standard EVA insoles.

The Effectiveness of Hard Insoles for Plantar Pressure in Cycling: A Crossover Study
PMC Open Access
Casado-Hernández et al. (2023) — PMC / NCBI — PMC10375994
Polypropylene insoles with aluminum metatarsal head reinforcement significantly decreased maximum plantar pressure across all measurement zones compared to standard EVA and polypropylene insoles. Wearing insoles inside cycling shoes produces plantar pressure redistribution — contoured insoles modify the contact area and plantar pressure distribution.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10375994
Claim 04

5 Million Knee Flexions Per Season

“A professional cyclist flexes their knee up to 5 million times per season” — Problem section
Directly supported — derived from published cycling load data

This figure is straightforward arithmetic from published data. A cyclist riding 20,000–30,000 km per season at an average cadence of 85–90 rpm produces approximately 5,000–5,700 pedal revolutions per hour (each producing two knee flexions — one per leg). At 20,000 km and an average speed of ~30 km/h, that's ~667 hours of riding × 5,400 pedal revs/hr × 2 = approximately 7.2 million individual knee flexions. The claim of “up to 5 million” is conservative and defensible.

Source: Cycling load data — foot–shoe–pedal interface research
PMC Open Access
O'Neil et al. (2016) — PMC4970853 — citing load calculation methodology
This paper and the systematic review by Bousie et al. establish that a trained cyclist can average up to 5,700 pedal revolutions in one hour — the basis for the seasonal load calculation cited on the VIANUcycle site. Custom cycling insoles for Toronto also references this range directly.
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4064526
Claim 05

Bike Fitters & the Insole Upsell

“A bike fit without foot analysis is built on sand” — Bike Fitter section
Supported by industry consensus and academic literature

This is not a quantified claim but a qualitative one supported across the literature. The systematic review by Bousie et al. explicitly states that "every bike fit is not complete without proper foot support" — a position shared by Retül (Trek's fitting system), leading independent fitters, and the Toronto cycling orthotics clinic literature. The G8 Performance trade publication documents bike fitters adding insoles as a revenue line without inventory investment — the exact model VIANUcycle enables.

Cycling Shoe Insoles: An Essential Add-On for Every Bike Fitting Service
Industry / Trade
G8 Performance (2025) — g8performance.com
Documents the business case for bike fitters: “Adding high-performance cycling shoe insoles to your product offering can create a new revenue stream while increasing the overall value of your fitting service.” Cyclists are always looking for ways to improve comfort and performance, making insoles a compelling upsell that complements an existing fitting package.
g8performance.com — Bike Fitter Guide
Retül + Masterfit: “A Bike Fit Is Not Complete Without Proper Foot Support”
Industry Precedent
Bicycle Retailer & Industry News (2014) — bicycleretailer.com
Retül (Trek's professional fitting system) partnered with Masterfit Enterprises to offer cycling-specific custom insoles as a complementary offering to their bike fit protocol. Todd Carver, Chief Fit Officer at Retül: “We believe that a bike fit is not complete without proper foot support.”
bicycleretailer.com — Retül 2014
